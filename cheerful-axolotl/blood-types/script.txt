One blood type quietly protects your heart. Another can be given to almost anyone on Earth, but can barely receive anything back. And there's one so rare that fewer than fifty people alive are known to carry it. It's called golden blood.

Today, every blood type gets a spot on this board. Eight types. Eight slots. Number one gets the crown. Every type has hidden advantages, and every single one has a price. By the end, you'll know exactly where yours landed. And trust me, the winner is not the one you think.

But first, a ten-second crash course. Your blood type is just two labels. The letter, A, B, AB, or O, tells you which tiny markers sit on your red blood cells. The plus or minus tells you whether you have one extra marker, called the Rh factor. That's it. Get blood with the wrong markers, and your immune system attacks it like an invader. Those tiny markers decide who can save your life, and whose life you can save.

Let's start with the rarest of all eight.

AB negative. Picture a blood bank with thousands of bags on the shelves. Now try to find yours. Good luck. Only about one in a hundred people has it. If you're one of them, here's your superpower. Your plasma, the yellow liquid part of your blood, can be given to patients of any blood type. Hospitals love you. If you know someone with AB negative, they're basically a rare collectible. But your body is far pickier. You can only receive red cells from other negative blood types, and those are already in short supply. On top of that, one large study found people with AB blood were around 80% more likely to develop memory and thinking problems later in life. Rare, generous, and a little complicated. AB negative lands right here. Number six.

Now, if you've ever heard the phrase "universal donor," this next one is the reason.

O negative. A car crash. A patient is losing blood fast. No time to test. Doctors grab one bag, and it's O negative, because its red cells can go into almost anybody. Only about 7% of people have it, and blood banks are constantly begging for more. Some air ambulances carry it on board for exactly this reason. You are, quite literally, an emergency hero. So, top three, right? Not quite. Because here's the cruel twist. When you need blood, you can only receive O negative. The type everyone else uses up. And if you're a woman with a negative type carrying a positive baby, your immune system can attack the baby's blood. Today, a simple injection prevents that, but it's one more thing to watch. Hero to everyone else, fragile on your own. Number five.

Notice that the hero didn't make the top three? Keep that in mind.

A positive. You're in surgery. Something goes wrong, and you need blood right now. Good news. You picked a popular type. About one in three people carry it. Your biggest advantage is simple. If you ever need blood, there's a lot of it out there. A positive, A negative, O positive, O negative. All safe for you. But A carries some quiet baggage. Studies link type A to roughly 20% higher risk of stomach cancer. And a big 2022 analysis found people with type A had a slightly higher risk of stroke before age sixty. Easy to save, harder to protect. A positive goes here. Number four.

Now let's flip to a type most people have never even met.

B negative. Imagine needing blood in a small town hospital, and the nurse comes back with an apologetic face. That's the B negative experience. Only about 2% of people have it. You can receive from B negative and O negative. That's the whole list. Two types, both scarce. Like every non-O type, you carry a bit more risk of heart disease and blood clots. And as a negative type, the pregnancy complication applies too. There's no special superpower here to balance the books. I really looked. You're not doomed. You're just playing on hard mode. So, B negative takes the bottom spot. Number eight.

Ouch. But the next type is the opposite. It can take almost anything.

AB positive. Imagine walking into any hospital in the world and knowing every bag of red blood cells on the shelf is safe for you. That's AB positive. The universal recipient. And your plasma? It can go to almost anyone too. Only about 3% of people have this type, and it's basically the VIP pass of blood. But nothing on this board is free. A Harvard study found people with AB blood had about 23% higher risk of heart disease than type O. Plus that memory link we saw earlier. Huge flexibility, real risks. AB positive goes high. Number two.

Number two is taken. Which means number one is still out there, and it's not the universal donor, and it's not the universal recipient. Keep guessing.

A negative. About 6% of people. There's some good news. Your red cells can go to A and AB types, positive or negative, so you're a pretty useful donor. But you also get all of type A's baggage, the stomach and stroke links, plus the negative-type limits. You can only receive A negative or O negative. Better options than B negative, but not by much. A negative goes here. Number seven.

Six slots filled. Two left. And one blood type that doesn't fit on this board at all.

Golden blood. Its real name is Rh-null. Remember the Rh factor? The Rh group is actually a family of more than fifty tiny markers. People with golden blood have none of them. Zero. The first known case was found in 1961, in an Aboriginal Australian woman. Since then, fewer than fifty people have ever been identified with it. Their blood can save people with extremely rare Rh types, which is why it's nicknamed golden. But if they need blood themselves, they can only receive Rh-null. From one of a few dozen people on the entire planet. Bags of it have been shipped across countries to reach a single patient. Many are told to bank their own blood, just in case. Too rare to rank. Golden blood gets a spot of its own, right off the board.

Back to the board. Two slots left. Number three, and the crown.

B positive. The quiet middle child of blood types. Not famous, not rare, just there. About 9% of people, and more common in parts of Asia. Your red cells can go to B positive and AB positive. You can receive from B and O types, positive or negative. That's a solid, balanced list. Heart disease risk? About 11% higher than type O. Better than AB, worse than O. And one large French study linked type B to a higher risk of type 2 diabetes. Solid, balanced, not perfect. B positive takes number three.

Which leaves one type. One slot. The crown.

And it's the one almost nobody bets on. Look at the person next to you on the bus. There's a good chance they have it. O positive. The most common blood type on the planet. Plain. Ordinary. Boring, right? Wrong. Type O is linked to the lowest risk of heart disease of all the groups, and a lower risk of dangerous blood clots. Lower links to stomach cancer than type A. And your red cells can be given to anyone with a positive type, which is most of humanity. That's why hospitals use more O positive than any other blood. It's the workhorse of the blood bank. Yes, there's a price. People with type O are more prone to stomach ulcers. They may bleed a little more easily. And one study even suggested mosquitoes prefer them. Annoying, but survivable. The most common blood on Earth, quietly the best. O positive. Number one.

So here's the final board. O positive on top. AB positive, B positive, A positive in the middle. O negative, AB negative, and A negative close behind. B negative at the bottom. And golden blood, in a league of its own.

Now, here's the part that really matters. Most people don't even know their own blood type. If you don't, find out. A simple blood test, or your first donation, will tell you. And if you're healthy enough, go donate. One donation can help save up to three lives, whatever slot you landed in.
