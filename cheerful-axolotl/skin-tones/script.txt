One skin tone comes with built-in sunscreen, roughly SPF 13, for free. Another makes vitamin D so efficiently it can survive a long, dark northern winter. And one of the most famous musicians in history lost his life to a skin cancer that started somewhere almost nobody checks. Under a toenail.

Here's the strange part. Almost every human, no matter their skin tone, has roughly the same number of melanin-making cells. Same number. So why does human skin come in so many shades? And why could knowing yours literally save your life?

Think of those cells as tiny paint factories. Everyone gets about the same number of factories. The difference is how much paint they make, and what kind. Dark brown paint, called eumelanin. Or reddish-yellow paint, called pheomelanin. That's it. That's the whole difference. And it all started in Africa. When our ancestors lost most of their body hair, their bare skin was suddenly exposed to brutal sunlight. So their factories cranked up the dark paint. Every human skin tone on Earth grew out of that moment.

Today, I'm taking six skin types, from the palest to the deepest, and placing each one on this world map, right where it works best. Each one has a hidden superpower. Each one pays a price. And by the end, the map is going to reveal something most people never notice.

Dermatologists actually sort skin into six types. It's called the Fitzpatrick scale, created in 1975 by a doctor who wanted to know how much UV light each patient could handle. Let's start with type one.

Type one. Very fair skin. You step outside on a summer afternoon, forget sunscreen, and twenty minutes later you look like a boiled lobster. You never tan. You just burn, peel, and start again. Huh? So where's the superpower? It's invisible. Your skin is a vitamin D machine. Vitamin D keeps your bones strong, and your skin makes it from sunlight. With so little melanin in the way, even weak winter sun gets straight through. That's why scientists think pale skin spread in places where sunlight is scarce. And a weird bonus. Many type ones carry gene variants linked to red hair, and studies suggest redheads may need around 20% more anesthesia during surgery. The price? The highest risk of melanoma and the fastest sun damage of any type. So, just avoid the sun completely? Problem solved? Not quite. Hide from it too much, and even you can run low on vitamin D. Solar-powered, but very easy to overcharge. Type one goes right here, the far north.

And if type one is the extreme, the next one is just a little less dramatic.

Type two. Fair skin that burns easily but can tan a little, if it's patient. Think of it as type one with a slightly better umbrella. And those freckles? They're tiny patches where your factories finally panicked and made some paint. Little emergency shields. You still make vitamin D easily in weak sunlight, a real advantage in cloudy, cold countries. But that thin umbrella doesn't hold up for long. Type two still burns, and still carries a high risk of skin cancer, especially the common kind called basal cell carcinoma. A little tougher, still fragile. Type two lands here. Northern lands, cool summers.

Now, the next type has a talent the first two would kill for. It can change.

Type three. Medium skin. You take a beach holiday, and instead of peeling, you come home golden. Your friends hate you. In winter, you're light. By the end of summer, you're two shades darker. Your skin is basically a seasonal wardrobe. When the sun gets stronger, your paint factories work overtime. When it fades, they slow down, and vitamin D production picks back up. That flexibility is your superpower. The price? You can still burn, and sun damage still adds up quietly over the years. Medium skin is also more prone to melasma, dark patches that often show up with hormone changes, like pregnancy. The switch hitter of skin. Type three goes here, right in the middle latitudes.

From here on, the sun starts losing the fight.

Type four. Olive or light brown skin. Your friends are hiding under umbrellas, reapplying sunscreen every hour. You're fine. You rarely burn, and you tan the moment the sun shows up. Your factories make plenty of dark eumelanin, and that pays off in a surprising way. Visible aging. Most wrinkles and spots on a face aren't caused by age. They're caused by the sun. More melanin means less damage, which means skin that often looks younger for longer. But here's the catch. When your skin gets hurt, a pimple, a scratch, a bug bite, the factories panic and overproduce. That spot can leave a dark mark that lasts for months. Doctors call it post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation. Strong shield, dramatic reactions. Type four goes here. Sunny, warm regions.

And if type four is strong, the next one guards something most people don't even know sunlight can steal.

Type five. Brown skin. Midday in a hot country, everyone's sprinting for shade. You stroll. You almost never burn. But your biggest superpower isn't about burning at all. It's folate. Folate is a B vitamin that matters enormously for building a healthy baby, and for making sperm. And sunlight breaks it down. Many scientists believe that protecting folate is one of the main reasons dark skin evolved in the first place. Your melanin is basically a bodyguard for the next generation. A bodyguard you never had to hire. Lower skin cancer rates, too. The price? Move to a cloudy, cold country, and that same shield blocks the sunlight you need for vitamin D. Deficiency becomes much more common. Brown skin is also more prone to keloids, raised scars that keep growing past the original wound. Bodyguard for life, with a winter weakness. Type five goes here. Strong sun, close to the tropics.

One type left. And it holds the strongest shield, and the most dangerous blind spot.

Type six. Deeply pigmented, dark brown to black skin. A whole day at an outdoor festival. No sunscreen. No hat. Not even a little pink. You never burn. Remember that built-in sunscreen? One classic study measured it. Very dark skin blocked UV with a natural SPF of around 13. Fair skin? Around 3. Skin cancer is much rarer. Wrinkles show up much later. And that folate protection runs at maximum. So, invincible? Not quite. Because skin cancer in dark skin is rare, it's often missed. It tends to appear where the sun barely reaches. Palms. Soles. Under the nails. In 1977, Bob Marley found a dark spot under his toenail. It was melanoma. He died four years later, at just 36. And there's more. Many medical textbooks mostly show rashes on light skin, so doctors can miss them on dark skin. Even the little finger clip that measures your oxygen, the pulse oximeter, can read too high on darker skin. The strongest shield on Earth, in a world that wasn't designed to see it. Type six goes here, right on the equator.
