Hour zero. You just finished a big meal. Your blood sugar rises, and your pancreas releases insulin, the hormone that tells your cells to grab that sugar and store the extra. Some goes into your liver as a quick-access fuel called glycogen. The rest gets packed into fat. Meanwhile, your gut sends a message to your brain. We're full. Scientists call it the migrating motor complex. Your gut is basically running a vacuum cleaner. Loudly. In a meeting.

And if that's the cleanup crew, the next stage is when your body starts raiding the pantry.

Hour eight to twelve. Your blood sugar dips. Insulin drops. So your liver opens the pantry and starts breaking down that stored glycogen, releasing sugar into your blood, nice and steady. Hour sixteen. This is the famous one. Sixteen hours without food, eight hours to eat. It's called sixteen-eight intermittent fasting, and millions of people swear by it. Your insulin is low. You're burning more fat. Your liver starts making small amounts of something called ketones, an emergency fuel made from fat. So, is this a magic weight-loss switch? Not quite. Studies suggest intermittent fasting mostly works because you end up eating less overall, and it seems to work about as well as regular dieting. The real advantage? Many people simply find it easier to stick to. No magic. Just a shorter window to snack in.

Hour thirty-six. You'd think the hunger keeps climbing. It doesn't. Hunger comes in waves, timed to when you normally eat. It's so important that the scientist who uncovered how it works won a Nobel Prize in 2016. But here's the honest part. Most of what we know about fasting and autophagy comes from animal studies. Day three. Seventy-two hours. Your brain is the biggest sugar addict in your body. But now, it adapts. In long fasts, ketones can supply up to around two thirds of your brain's energy. Beyond day three. Fasting for several days isn't a lifestyle hack anymore. It's a medical situation. Mineral levels can drop dangerously, which can disturb your heart rhythm. Long fasts can raise the risk of gallstones. And here's the most dangerous part of all. Ending the fast. After a long fast, eating too much, too fast, can trigger something called refeeding syndrome. Your minerals shift so suddenly that it can damage the heart and even kill. That's why people coming off long fasts are fed slowly, under medical care. The scariest part of a long fast isn't the fast. It's the first meal.

Which brings us back to the man I promised you.

In 1965, a twenty-seven-year-old Scottish man named Angus Barbieri weighed about four hundred and fifty-six pounds. He went to a hospital in Dundee and asked doctors to help him fast. They planned a few days. He kept going. And going. For three hundred and eighty-two days, he ate no food at all. He drank water, tea, coffee, and sparkling water, and doctors gave him vitamins and minerals. He was checked constantly. By the end, he weighed about one hundred and eighty pounds. He'd lost roughly two hundred and seventy-six. It's still the longest recorded fast in history. And his first meal after more than a year? A boiled egg, and a slice of bread with butter. He said he'd forgotten what food tasted like. Doctors still study his case today. And every single one of them would tell you the same thing. Do not try this.

So, here's what fasting really does. It flips your body from storing energy to burning it. It cleans your gut, raises your growth hormone, switches your brain to a backup fuel, and might help your cells recycle themselves. Short fasts, like skipping a late-night snack or trying sixteen-eight, are safe for most healthy adults. But fasting isn't for everyone. If you're pregnant, under eighteen, have diabetes, take medication, or have ever struggled with an eating disorder, talk to a doctor first. Your body is smart. Don't make it prove it.
