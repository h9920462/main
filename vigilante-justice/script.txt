Today we're looking at people who got tired of waiting for justice and decided to deliver it themselves. One of these stories ends in a courtroom. One ends on a gallows. And one ends in a bakery. Let's start with the courtroom.

The Women of Kasturba Nagar.

It's the summer of 2004 in Nagpur, India. A young woman named Usha Narayane is walking home through Kasturba Nagar, the crowded neighborhood where she grew up. Usha is one of the only people on her street who's been to college. She has a degree in hotel management, a plan for her future, and a family who couldn't be prouder of her. Life, for once, is looking up.

Then she turns onto her street, and something's wrong.

Everyone is staring at the ground. Doors are closing. Mothers are pulling their kids inside. Nobody says a word. Because walking the other way down the road is the one man everyone in Kasturba Nagar has learned to fear.

His name is Bharat Kalicharan. But everyone calls him Akku Yadav.

For more than ten years, Akku and his gang have run this neighborhood like their own private kingdom. He squeezes money out of shop owners. He robs families. He's been accused of murdering at least three people. But the worst accusations are about the women. Dozens of them say he assaulted them, many inside their own homes.

So why doesn't anyone stop him? Because the women who go to the police learn a painful lesson very quickly. Akku pays the local cops, and the cops return the favor. When a woman reports him, officers tip him off. And then Akku shows up at her door to punish her for talking. He gets arrested again and again. And again and again, he's back on the street within days.

So the neighborhood survives by one rule. Keep your head down, and pray he never notices you.

Mothers stop letting their daughters walk to the market alone. Families who can afford it pack up and leave. And the ones who can't? They learn to live with a monster down the street, knowing the people paid to stop him are the same people he's paying.

But Usha has never been good at keeping her head down.

When Akku's men threaten a neighbor's family, Usha helps them write a formal complaint and marches it straight to the police. Word gets back to Akku almost instantly. And a few days later, he's standing outside Usha's house with a mob of around 40 men behind him.

He threatens to throw acid in her face. He promises to do things to her that the whole neighborhood will hear about. Usha slams the door and locks it. Fists pound on the walls. The whole house shakes.

And then Usha does something nobody expects.

She runs to the kitchen, grabs her family's gas cylinder, and twists the valve wide open. Gas hisses into the room. Then she screams through the door: if anyone takes one step inside, she'll light a match and take every single one of them with her.

Outside, the neighbors hear everything. For years, they've listened to screams like this through thin walls and done nothing. But tonight is different. One by one, doors open. People step outside. Then dozens. Then hundreds. They pick up sticks. They pick up stones. And for the first time in over a decade, Akku Yadav's gang turns and runs.

The crowd doesn't stop there. They march to Akku's house and burn it to the ground. And for the first time anyone can remember, Akku Yadav is the one who's afraid.

So he does what any reasonable criminal would do. He walks into a police station and asks to be arrested. For his own protection. Which honestly tells you everything you need to know about the police in Nagpur.

His bail hearing is set for August 13th, 2004. And almost immediately, a rumor starts moving through Kasturba Nagar. Akku has already paid off the right people. When he walks into that courthouse, he's going to walk right back out a free man.

The women of the neighborhood talk it over. And they all reach the same conclusion. If he gets out, he's coming back. And this time, he'll make every one of them pay.

On the afternoon of August 13th, Akku Yadav is led into the Nagpur district courthouse. He's smirking. Joking with the officers. Totally relaxed, like a man who already knows how this ends. Walking down the corridor, he spots one of the women he'd assaulted standing near the courtroom door. He sneers at her and threatens to do it again.

She pulls off her sandal and smacks him across the face with it.

"It's you or me," she screams. "We can't both live on this earth."

And then the doors burst open.

Around 200 women flood into the courthouse from every direction. Hidden in their clothes are kitchen knives and handfuls of chili powder. They hurl the powder into the eyes of the police officers, who stagger backwards, blinded, and run. And then every woman in that room turns toward Akku.

For years, he'd walked into their homes like he owned them. Now he's alone, in a room full of the people he hurt, and there's no one left to protect him.

He's screaming now. Begging. Swearing he'll never do it again. But the women of Kasturba Nagar have heard his promises before. They surround him and start stabbing, over and over. He tries to crawl under a bench. There's nowhere to go. His screams echo down the halls of the courthouse, and nobody comes to help. The floor turns slick with blood. Every woman there makes sure she lands at least one blow.

About fifteen minutes later, Akku Yadav lies dead on the courtroom floor, with more than 70 stab wounds in his body.

Police arrest several women, including Usha, even though she insists she was nowhere near the courthouse. But as soon as the news breaks, something incredible happens. Hundreds of women from Kasturba Nagar march down to the police station. And every single one of them says the exact same thing.

"We all did it. Arrest all of us."

The case drags on for years. Nobody can prove whose hand struck which blow. And in November 2014, a full decade later, the court acquits every single person charged with Akku Yadav's murder.

It turns out that when the police won't protect a neighborhood, sometimes the neighborhood protects itself.

But what happens when the man who's supposed to protect you is the one you need protecting from? For that, we need to go back 150 years, to the American gold rush.

Henry Plummer.

It's 1856 in Nevada City, California, and 24-year-old Henry Plummer has just been elected town marshal. In a gold rush town packed with drunk miners and short tempers, Plummer is exactly what people wanted. He's young. He's handsome. He's soft-spoken, always in a clean suit, and he can draw a pistol faster than anyone in town. People like him so much, there's even talk of him running for the state legislature one day.

On paper, he's the perfect lawman. But people are about to learn he has a habit of killing anyone who gets in his way.

In 1857, the town starts noticing that their marshal is spending an awful lot of time at the home of a married woman. Her husband, John Vedder, is not a fan of this arrangement. And one night, Vedder ends up dead with Plummer's bullets inside him. Plummer claims self-defense. The jury doesn't buy it. He's convicted of second-degree murder and sentenced to 10 years in San Quentin.

He serves less than two.

In 1859, he convinces the governor he's dying of tuberculosis and walks out with a pardon. Spoiler: he does not die of tuberculosis. Instead, he heads straight back to Nevada City, kills another man in a brawl, bribes his way out of jail, and disappears north.

By 1862, Plummer drifts into a brand-new gold rush camp called Bannack, in what would soon become Montana. Bannack is a muddy, lawless mess of tents, saloons, and log cabins. And the best part, for Plummer, is that nobody there knows a thing about his past. In January of 1863, he shoots a man named Jack Cleveland dead in a saloon. And somehow, instead of ruining his reputation, the killing kind of impresses people.

A few months later, the miners of Bannack hold an election for sheriff.

"So we need a sheriff. Anybody got a problem with the handsome guy who shot Jack Cleveland?"

"I mean, he did shoot him really well."

"Good enough for me."

"Wait, didn't he also go to prison for murder in California?"

"That's California's problem."

Plummer wins. A man with two murders and a prison record is now the law in Bannack. That summer, he even gets married. His new wife leaves him after about three months, which in hindsight might have been the first red flag anyone in Montana actually noticed.

Because something strange is starting to happen around Bannack.

The roads between the gold camps are becoming some of the most dangerous in the entire West. Stagecoaches are being held up. Miners carrying gold dust vanish somewhere between towns, and their bodies turn up weeks later at the bottom of ravines. And here's the creepy part. The masked robbers always seem to know exactly which coach is carrying gold, and exactly when it's leaving. By some counts, more than a hundred people are robbed or killed on those roads in less than a year.

And Sheriff Plummer never seems to catch anybody. Which is weird, because he's supposed to be really good at this.

In December of 1863, a young man named Nicholas Thiebalt heads out to pick up a pair of mules he'd just paid for. He never comes back. Days later, a hunter finds his frozen body in the brush. There's a bullet in his head and rope marks on his neck. He'd been dragged through the brush while he was still alive.

This time, the miners have had enough.

A posse tracks down the man last seen with Thiebalt, a gang member named George Ives, and drags him in front of a miners' court. The trial is held outdoors, in the snow, in front of more than a thousand people. Ives is found guilty. When he asks for a little more time to write a letter to his mother, someone in the crowd shouts back: "Ask him how much time he gave the Dutchman." Within the hour, George Ives is hanging from a beam.

Two days later, on December 23rd, a group of businessmen meets in secret in Virginia City. They swear an oath. They're done waiting for the law. And they form a committee called the Vigilantes of Montana.

In early January, they catch a gang member named Red Yeager. With the rope waiting for him, Red decides to talk. And what he says stuns everyone in the room.

These road agents aren't random outlaws. They're an organized gang of dozens of men, and they recognize each other with a secret password: "I am innocent." And their leader, the man planning every robbery and passing along which coaches are carrying gold, is none other than Sheriff Henry Plummer.

The vigilantes hang Red Yeager on January 4th, 1864. Then they ride for Bannack.

But news of the confession travels faster than they do. Some of the men on that list saddle up and run. Plummer doesn't. He's the sheriff, after all. Who would dare?

On the freezing night of January 10th, 1864, armed men drag Henry Plummer out of the house where he's staying. They march him and two of his deputies through the snow to the edge of town. And standing there, dark against the winter sky, is a gallows.

The same gallows Sheriff Plummer built himself, just months earlier, to hang a prisoner.

This is the moment his famous calm finally cracks. He drops to his knees in the snow and begs. He offers them his gold. He swears he'll leave the territory forever. He tells them to cut off his ears, cut out his tongue, anything but this. When none of it works, he asks for one final favor.

"Give me a good drop."

His deputies go first. Then the noose slips over Plummer's head, and Henry Plummer hangs from his own gallows.

And the vigilantes are just getting started. Over the next six weeks, they hang more than 20 men across Montana. Some were almost certainly murderers. Some might have just been in the wrong saloon at the wrong time. None of them got a real trial.

To warn any outlaws still in the territory, the vigilantes begin leaving a strange set of numbers on doors and tents: three, seven, seventy-seven. To this day, nobody knows for sure what those numbers mean. But if you ever get pulled over in Montana, take a look at the trooper's shoulder. The Montana Highway Patrol still wears 3-7-77 on its patch.

Oh, and one more thing. In 1993, Henry Plummer finally got his day in court, in a mock trial held in Virginia City. After hearing all the evidence, the jury split six to six. Which means if Henry Plummer had actually been given a trial back in 1864, there's a decent chance he would have walked free.

Again.

Those two stories were about revenge on a single man. Our last story is about a group of people who wanted revenge on an entire country. And they got closer than you might think.

Abba Kovner.

It's June of 1941 in Vilna, a city in what's now Lithuania. Twenty-three-year-old Abba Kovner looks up as the roar of German planes fills the sky.

Abba is an art student and a leader in a Jewish youth group. The teenagers in his group look up to him like a big brother. Vilna is home to around 60,000 Jews, and the city is so famous for its scholars and libraries that people call it the Jerusalem of Lithuania.

Within three years, almost all of them will be dead.

As the Nazis take the city, Abba escapes to a convent just outside Vilna. There, a group of Catholic nuns disguise him and several of his friends and hide them from the Germans. But even behind those walls, terrible rumors start to reach him. Every day, the Germans are rounding up thousands of Jews in Vilna and telling them they're being sent to work camps. They're marched to a forest called Ponary, a few miles outside the city.

And none of them ever come back.

Then a survivor stumbles back into the city. A young woman who had been shot and buried along with everyone else, and who had crawled out from under the bodies after dark. She tells anyone who will listen: there are no work camps. At Ponary, the Germans are lining people up at the edges of huge pits and shooting them.

Most people refuse to believe her. It's too horrible to be true. Some even accuse her of spreading panic.

But Abba believes her. And in that moment, he understands something most people around him can't accept yet. The Nazis don't plan to enslave the Jews of Europe. They plan to kill every last one of them.

On New Year's Eve, as 1941 turns into 1942, Abba stands in front of about 150 young people crammed into a room in the ghetto. He reads out a manifesto he's written. Hitler plans to destroy all the Jews of Europe, he tells them.

"Let us not go like sheep to the slaughter. We must fight back."

A few weeks later, he helps form one of the first armed Jewish resistance groups in any Nazi ghetto. For almost two years, they smuggle guns in piece by piece and hide them inside walls and under floorboards. Then, in September 1943, the Germans move to wipe out the ghetto for good.

Abba leads his fighters down into the city's sewers. For hours, they crawl through pitch-black tunnels, wading through filth, until they finally climb out beyond the city and escape into the forest. There, Abba commands a unit of Jewish partisans who blow up German trains and ambush German soldiers.

And his unit has a name. The Avengers.

In July 1944, the Soviets drive the Germans out of Vilna, and Abba marches back into his city. He walks through the empty streets of the ghetto, past the courtyards where his youth group used to meet. The apartments are silent. The doors hang open. Almost every one of the teenagers who looked up to him is gone. Out of the roughly 60,000 Jews who lived in Vilna, only a few thousand are still alive.

Over the next year, he travels across Poland and sees the camps with his own eyes. The gas chambers. The mountains of shoes. And he hears the number that everyone is starting to hear.

Six million.

Then the war ends. And Abba watches the world simply move on. Most Nazis take off their uniforms and go home. Only a small fraction ever face trial. Former SS men slip back into their old lives as if nothing happened. Some even get their old jobs back as policemen, teachers, and government officials. Meanwhile, hundreds of thousands of survivors are still living in camps, with no homes and no families left to return to.

And something inside Abba Kovner hardens into one terrifying idea. If the world won't make Germany pay, the survivors will. A life for a life. Six million Germans for six million Jews.

In 1945, he gathers around 50 fellow survivors, many of them former partisans, and forms a secret group called Nakam, the Hebrew word for revenge. Their first plan, which they call Plan A, is one of the most horrifying revenge plots in history. They're going to poison the water supply of major German cities. And they're going to start with Nuremberg, the city where the Nazis held their giant rallies and passed their race laws.

Nakam members slip into Germany under fake names. One of them gets hired at Nuremberg's water company and quietly gets hold of maps of the city's pipes, including the exact valves where poison could be added to reach the most homes. For months, the avengers study the city, learning its streets and its pipes, living side by side with the people they're planning to kill.

Everything is ready. There's just one thing missing. The poison.

So Abba travels to British-controlled Palestine to get it. Most of the Jewish leaders he speaks to there are horrified by the plan and refuse to help. But according to Abba's own account, a young chemist named Ephraim Katzir helps him get it. Decades later, that chemist would become the president of Israel. Which is definitely not something you put on the campaign poster.

In December of 1945, Abba boards a British ship back to Europe, disguised as a soldier, with the poison hidden in cans inside his bag. The ship crosses the Mediterranean. Everything is going to plan. Then, as it nears the French port of Toulon, the ship's loudspeaker crackles to life.

And it calls out his name.

Abba Kovner is ordered to report to the ship's officers. Immediately.

He's been found out. Abba turns to his travel companion and gives him one quick order. If I don't come back, throw the bag overboard. Abba is arrested. His companion drops the poison into the sea. And Plan A, the plot meant to kill millions, sinks quietly to the bottom of the Mediterranean.

Abba spends the next several months in British prisons. Back in Germany, the rest of Nakam is left with no leader and no poison.

But they aren't giving up. They're just changing targets.

Just outside Nuremberg sits Stalag 13-D, an American prison camp holding thousands of German prisoners. Many of them are former members of the SS, the organization that ran the death camps. Every day, the camp gets its bread from a large bakery in Nuremberg. So Plan B is simple. If they can't poison Germany, they'll poison the SS.

A Nakam member named Leipke Distel gets a job at that bakery. For weeks, he's the perfect employee. Friendly. Hard-working. Always happy to pick up an extra shift. And little by little, the group smuggles arsenic into the building and hides it where no one will look.

On the night of April 13th, 1946, while the bakery is closed, Distel and a few others slip inside. They mix the arsenic into a paste. Then, loaf by loaf, they paint the poison onto the bottoms of the bread meant for the SS prisoners. They work for hours in total silence, freezing at every sound from the street outside. One wrong noise and it's over. There's no explaining a bag of arsenic in a bakery at two in the morning.

By the time they have to stop, they've coated around 3,000 loaves. They slip out before sunrise, cross the border, and flee Germany, leaving nothing behind but the bread sitting quietly on the bakery shelves.

The next day, that bread is served at the camp.

Within hours, prisoners start doubling over in agony. Vomiting. Convulsing. Barracks turn into makeshift hospital wards. The guards have no idea what's going on. Is it food poisoning? Some kind of disease? Before long, more than 2,000 prisoners are sick. The Americans rush the worst cases to hospitals and launch an investigation, and newspapers around the world report on a mysterious mass poisoning of German prisoners.

The members of Nakam are convinced they've killed hundreds. Maybe thousands.

But the official record doesn't list a single confirmed death.

Decades later, declassified documents revealed that investigators found enough arsenic in the bakery to kill around 60,000 people. One theory is that the avengers spread the poison too thin, trying to cover as many loaves as they possibly could.

Plan B had failed. Nakam slowly falls apart, and most of its members move to Palestine to start new lives.

And Abba Kovner? After his release, he settles on a kibbutz and becomes one of the most celebrated poets in Israel. In 1961, he takes the stand at the trial of Adolf Eichmann, one of the architects of the Holocaust, and testifies about what happened in Vilna. In 1970, he receives the Israel Prize for literature, one of the country's highest honors. He dies in 1987.

For decades, the story of Nakam stays a whispered secret. Then, in 1999, two surviving members go on German television and openly admit to the bread poisoning. German prosecutors open an investigation for attempted murder.

A year later, they quietly drop it, citing the unusual circumstances of the case.
